# Retraining Protocol

2026-11-02 · @Chris

## How the plan works

Starting Strength's linear progression is the engine: three lifting days a week, with weight added every session for as long as that keeps working. Barbell Medicine supplies two things: conditioning that starts small and builds slowly, and a reps-in-reserve scale for sizing each jump.

Your Modular Eating System runs alongside it. A deficit means lifts will stall sooner than Starting Strength expects, so this plan puts a ladder of fixes in front of every reset. Rules that come from this plan rather than from either program are marked as this plan's.

**Before you train hard.** Under ACSM's screening guidance, someone returning from inactivity with no known heart, metabolic, or kidney disease and no symptoms can start at light to moderate effort and build gradually ([screening guidance](https://www.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/-/media/files/student-hub/research-ethics/carnegie-health-screening-guidance.pdf)). That's why intervals wait until week 3. With a known heart, metabolic, or kidney condition, get medical clearance before heavy lifting or intervals. Stop the session, and get checked before training again, if you have chest pain or pressure, fainting, unusual breathlessness, dizziness, or a racing or irregular heartbeat during or after effort. Chest pain that doesn't ease within a few minutes of stopping is a 911 call. Ankle swelling, calf pain on short walks, or breathlessness lying flat call for a doctor's visit before you train. If your blood pressure hasn't been checked this year, get it checked before the weights get heavy (this plan's suggestion).

Heavy soreness in the first two weeks is normal after a year off. Joint pain, sharp pain, or pain that changes how you move is not. If it starts during a set, stop the set, skip the rest of that lift, and finish the session with the lifts that don't hurt. Log it as stopped for pain, not as a miss. If the pain is gone by the next session, work that lift at 90% of its last weight for one session, then pick up where you left off. If it's still there in the next session's warm-ups, skip that lift and see a doctor or physical therapist before loading it again (this plan's rule).

## The week

Lift Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. Do conditioning Tuesday and Thursday. Weekends are off, and every session fits inside 80 minutes.

| Day | Session | Time (min) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Lifting: Workout A or B | 45–75 |
| Tuesday | Conditioning | 20–60, by phase |
| Wednesday | Lifting: the other workout | 45–75 |
| Thursday | Conditioning | 20–60, by phase |
| Friday | Lifting: same workout as Monday | 45–75 |

The workouts alternate, so one week runs A, B, A and the next runs B, A, B. You ran A on Monday, November 2, which makes this Wednesday a B day and Friday an A day. The next workout is always the opposite of the last one you completed, whatever the day, and a training week runs Monday through Sunday.

If you miss a lifting day, you can move it to the next weekday as long as you don't lift two days in a row, and that day's conditioning drops. Otherwise skip it. Either way, keep the A/B order, and the next session gets the weights the missed one would have had (this plan's rule).

Lifting days take about 45 minutes now and stretch toward 75 as the weights climb, mostly because rests get longer. Rest between work sets until you can make a clean set: about 2 minutes while sets end with 3 or more reps to spare, 3 to 5 minutes once they end with 2 or fewer, and at least 5 minutes between sets of 3.

If a lifting day is heading past 80 minutes, cut in this order (this plan's rule):

1. Skip the easy spin after lifting.
2. Drop one warm-up set from each lift.
3. Do chin sets in the rests between press or bench sets, with the dips after the last one (see Supersets and trisets).

Never shorten the rest between squat or deadlift work sets.

## The lifting sessions

Each session runs squat, a press, a pull, and then a short chin-and-dip block, following Starting Strength's opening phases.

| Slot | Workout A | Workout B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Squat 3×5 | Squat 3×5 |
| 2 | Press 3×5 | Bench press 3×5 |
| 3 | Deadlift 1×5 | Weeks 1–2: deadlift 1×5. From week 3: power clean 5×3 or barbell row 3×5 |
| 4 | Chins and dips, 3 rounds of 5 each | Chins and dips, 3 rounds of 5 each |

Start with 5 easy minutes on the air bike as a warm-up, which doesn't count as conditioning. For each lift, do two sets of 5 with the empty bar (for the deadlift, start at 135 with 45-lb plates, or with the bar raised to that height), then two to four sets that close the gap to the work weight, cutting reps as you go (5, then 3, then 2). Work sets are "across," with the same weight for every set.

**Bench and dips start Wednesday.** For bench, do sets of 5 from the empty bar, adding 20 lb a set while the bar moves fast and 10 lb a set once it starts to slow. The set where it clearly slows is your first work set, so do two more at that weight. For dips, find the assistance that gives 5 solid reps with 2 or 3 to spare, stopping when your upper arms reach parallel.

**Power cleans are optional.** Andrew Lewis, who runs Starting Strength Indianapolis, says to clean if you can, if you want to, and if cleans don't hurt your slow lifts or daily life ([thread](https://startingstrength.com/resources/forum/general-q-and-a/91794-power-clean-post1806907.html)). Explosive strength is also the quality cardio blunts most ([Schumann et al.](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/71546/1/Schumann2021_Article_CompatibilityOfConcurrentAerob.pdf)). If you don't already clean well, row instead of learning cleans from video.

**Wednesday, November 4.** Size the jumps by how Monday's last work set felt (full table in the next section).

| Lift (lb) | Monday | Felt light, 5+ left | 3–4 left | 1–2 left | A grind, 0 left |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Squat | 95 | 115 | 105 | 100 | 97.5 |
| Deadlift | 145 | 175 | 160 | 155 | 150 |
| Press (Friday) | 65 | 75 | 70 | 67.5 | 66 |

Chins move to 155 lb of assistance if all 15 of Monday's reps came with a rep or two to spare, or to 157.5 if they came with none to spare. Friday's press is sized by Monday's last press set.

## How fast to add weight

Add weight every session, sized by the last work set of that lift's last heavy session, since light days, light weeks, and floor weeks don't set jumps. Judge that set by its slowest rep: how many more clean reps could you have done?

| Last work set felt | Squat (lb) | Deadlift (lb) | Press (lb) | Bench (lb) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Light, 5+ reps left | +20 | +30 | +10 | +10 |
| 3–4 reps left | +10 | +15 | +5 | +5 |
| 1–2 reps left | +5 | +10 | +2.5 | +5 |
| A grind, 0 left | +2.5 | +5 | +1 | +2.5 |

Missed reps go to the stall ladder below. Power cleans and rows add 5 lb a session until the first session with a slow or missed rep, then 2.5 lb for the rest of the novice run.

The table is this plan's rule. It pairs Starting Strength's bar-speed cue with the reps-in-reserve scale behind Barbell Medicine's RPE, where an RPE 8 set has 2 reps left. Neither program prints these numbers.

Aim for most work sets to end with a rep or two left. A 2024 set of meta-regressions found strength gains barely depend on how close to failure sets end, while load matters more ([Robinson et al.](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4089104?lng=en)). Grinds cost recovery without buying much.

When in doubt, take the smaller jump. You'll need fractional plates (0.5 and 1.25 lb) for the small jumps, and the press will need them first. Round every calculated weight down to what your plates can load. Without fractional plates, the smallest jump is 5 lb, so bring your own set.

## Chins and dips

Chins and dips are paired every session: 3 rounds of one chin set and one dip set, 5 reps each, on the assist machine. Rippetoe's rule when you can't chin yet is to get as close to the chin as you can, and his tool for that is the lat machine ([The Lat Machine](https://startingstrength.com/training/the-lat-machine)). This plan uses the assist machine instead, since your body still moves (this plan's choice).

1. **All 15 reps.** Take 5 lb of assistance off next session if you had a rep or more to spare, or 2.5 lb if you had none. If the stack moves in 10s, stack magnets give you the half-steps, as Rippetoe recommends for the lat machine.
2. **Missed reps.** Repeat that setting, then switch to 2.5 lb steps.
3. **Even 2.5 lb steps stall.** That means two misses in a row at one setting. Go back to the last setting you made, and add a rep to each set every session you make them all, from 3 sets of 6 to 3 sets of 8. Then take 5 lb off and go back to sets of 5. This is this plan's version of [Barbell Logic's](https://barbell-logic.com/?p=2969) band progression, which builds to 10 reps on the first set before cutting assistance.
4. **Every rep.** Lower over about 3 seconds. The machine makes slow negatives possible at any setting.
5. **At 50 lb of assistance or less.** Add 2 bodyweight negatives after the last chin set on A days, or on your chin days if you've split the pair. By then you're strong enough to control the descent (this plan's threshold).
6. **Zero assistance.** Do as many reps as you can in each of 3 sets, up to 5, and add a rep a session until you reach 3×5. Then add 2.5 lb on a belt each session you make it.

Your real load is bodyweight minus assistance. Each pound the scale drops makes the same setting easier, which is free progress toward your first chin.

For dips, lower until your upper arms are parallel to the floor, and no deeper, for the first four weeks. After that you may go until your shoulders are just below your elbows, never past the point where your shoulders roll forward (this plan's rule). If elbows or shoulders feel achy after chins and dips, without the pain described under How the plan works, split the pair: chins on B days, dips on A days.

If the assist machine is down, do lat pulldowns for 3 sets of 8 with 2 reps to spare, and incline push-ups for 3 sets with 2 reps to spare in place of dips. Those sessions don't change your assistance settings (this plan's rule).

## Supersets and trisets

Superset the assistance work from day one. Keep barbell work sets straight, with full rest, for as long as you're adding weight every session.

The rests aren't dead time. In a linear progression each work set sits near your current limit, and the program only moves if you finish every rep. Fatigue carried over from a paired exercise costs reps, and missed reps are how a run stalls.

The research fits that split. A 2025 meta-analysis found supersets cut session time without cutting volume ([Zhang et al.](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/11908/1/SupersetVersusTraditionalResistanceTrainingPrescriptionsASystematicReviewAndMetaanalysisExploringAcuteAndChronicPV-WEAKLEY.pdf)). Long-term strength and muscle gains matched traditional sets, though only three studies tracked the long term. Supersets also felt harder and raised blood lactate, and pairing the same muscles cut volume while pairing opposing muscles held it.

Rippetoe is relaxed about the chin itself ([thread](https://startingstrength.com/resources/forum/general-q-and-a/98932-chinups-programmed-advanced-novice-lp-20-chins-post1905260.html)): "Chins are an assistance exercise. Do them any way you want to."

| Pairing | When | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chins with dips | Every session, starting now | Pull and push use different muscles |
| Chin, dip, and one light extra (a triset) | Only if you add a third exercise | Keep it light and unrelated, such as back extensions or hanging knee raises. The plan doesn't need one. |
| Chin sets between press or bench sets | Days heading past 80 minutes | Opposing muscles. Keep at least 3 minutes between press sets. |
| Anything between squat or deadlift sets | Not during the novice run | Both lifts tax the whole body, and they drive the program |
| Conditioning in a lifting session | After lifting, never before | Lifting first gave better leg-strength gains in a 2018 meta-analysis ([Eddens et al.](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s40279-017-0784-1)) |

After the novice run, when progress goes weekly, lighter days and accessory work leave more room for pairing.

## Conditioning

Conditioning builds over about 10 weeks to 100 to 120 minutes a week on Tuesday and Thursday, plus up to 30 minutes of easy spinning after lifting. With the spins, it reaches about 150 minutes a week, the floor of the federal activity guidelines ([Piercy et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9582631)), and then holds there.

Easy work goes on the air bike, at a pace where you can talk in full sentences but couldn't sing. Intervals go on the SkiErg, which spares the legs you squat with three times a week. One interval is 30 seconds hard but not all-out, about 8 out of 10, where you could get out only a few words, then 90 seconds easy. The intervals sit in the middle of the session, inside its time.

| Weeks | Tuesday | Thursday | After lifting (M/W/F) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1–2 | 20–30 min easy | 20–30 min easy | None |
| 3–4 | 30–40 min easy | 30–40 min easy, 6 intervals in the middle | None |
| 5–8 | 40–50 min easy | 40–50 min easy, 8 intervals | 10 min easy, if time allows |
| 9 on | 50–60 min easy | 50–60 min easy, 8–10 intervals | 10 min easy, if time allows |

Count conditioning weeks, not calendar weeks. A week counts when you do both sessions, and light weeks, floor weeks, and missed weeks don't count, so time off never pushes you into harder work. Within a phase, start at the low end and add 5 minutes a week up to the top. After more than three weeks without conditioning, drop back a phase, and after more than two months, start again at week 1 (this plan's rule).

Short on time, do what you can, down to 20 easy minutes, and it still counts. Skip the intervals if you have less than 30 minutes. Conditioning days get an extra slot of food (see Eating and sleep).

This ramp is this plan's own. It starts with two short easy sessions and lengthens them instead of adding days. Barbell Medicine's Beginner Prescription, as a forum member describes it, starts with one easy session and builds to two easy and two interval sessions a week, and Jordan Feigenbaum's reply favors easy work, "3 LISS per week and 1 HIIT" ([forum](https://forum.barbellmedicine.com/t/starting-with-4-conditioning-sessions/14722)). This plan leans on easy work for the same reason. Austin Baraki writes that HIIT's unique benefits "have likely been overstated" and that hard intervals carry "a higher fatigue cost" ([forum](https://forum.barbellmedicine.com/t/when-to-begin-conditioning/1944)).

This is the ceiling. Don't add sessions to pay for a big meal. Your eating plan sets the same rule, and extra drive goes into lifting well.

If intervals leave you flat for the next lifting day, halve the rounds the next week, rounding down. If the SkiErg bothers elbows or shoulders that already do chins, dips, and presses, or it's taken or broken, move intervals to the air bike. If the air bike is down, walk briskly on an incline treadmill.

## When a lift stalls

A lift has stalled when you miss reps at the same weight twice in a row. A set is missed when any rep falls short of full depth or lockout or needs help, and finishing the reps in broken sets still counts as a miss. A lift you couldn't finish because you had to leave, or didn't reach because time ran out, isn't a miss. It repeats its weight next time. A lift stopped for pain isn't a miss either, and it follows the pain rule under How the plan works. Each lift runs its own ladder. When a lift makes all its reps again, its next jump is its smallest (the grind row), and from then on it never jumps more than its 1–2 left row (this plan's rule).

Two changes come first, once a lift's jumps have been 5 lb or less two sessions in a row:

- **Squat.** Make the week's middle session, normally Wednesday, a light day at 80% of your last heavy squat, for 2 sets of 5 ([Alter](https://startingstrength.com/article/the-reset-why-and-how)). The other two sessions keep climbing. In a week with only two sessions, both are heavy, and light days never count toward a stall.
- **Deadlift.** Pull heavy once a week, at the week's first A session. If that session is missed, pull at the next A session that week, and if there isn't one, skip the week. Any other A day that week gets the power cleans or rows you chose for B days. This change can't start before week 3 (this plan's rule).

Then the ladder:

1. **Miss reps once.** Repeat the weight. One bad day isn't a stall, so check sleep, food, and rest times.
2. **Miss again at that weight.** Next time, do 5×3 at the same weight: the same 15 reps in shorter sets, with at least 5 minutes between sets. For the deadlift, do 2×3 ([Alter](https://startingstrength.com/article/the-reset-why-and-how)). Power cleans are already sets of 3, so they go straight to step 4.
3. **Then 3×5 at that weight (1×5 for the deadlift).** Make it and you're climbing again. Miss it, and the squat runs the pair once more while the other lifts go to step 4. Light days don't count toward a round, and a light or floor week pauses the count.
4. **Still stuck, or you miss the short sets.** Reset to 90% of the weight that stalled, rounded down to the nearest 5 lb for the deadlift and 2.5 lb for the rest, and climb back.
5. **Stall again at or below the weight that forced the reset.** The novice run is over for that lift alone. Hold it at its last completed weight and sets until you pick its next program, such as Starting Strength's Texas Method or a Barbell Medicine template (see What research on trained lifters adds), and keep the other lifts here. A stall above that weight runs the ladder again.

Steps 2 and 3 are the 5×3 bridge. Starting Strength's reset guidance supplies the pieces but not this order ([Alter](https://startingstrength.com/article/the-reset-why-and-how)). On a first miss, Alter adds rest, allows broken sets, and switches to sets of 3 when the first sets come up well short. After a true stall she resets 5% and adds a light squat day, and a lift that stalls again moves to new programming, only that lift. The bridge rounds and the 10% reset are this plan's rules. A 10% drop gives a lifter in a deficit more easy sessions before the old weight, at the cost of a longer climb back.

**Check the eating plan before step 4.** If a lift would reach step 4 in the 14 days before a maintenance break (December 7 to 20, 2026, or February 15 to 28, 2027), keep running the 5×3 and 3×5 pair at that weight until the break, then retry the normal sets at the break's first heavy session (December 28 for the first break, after the Christmas floor week). If a lift reaches step 4 in the first eight weeks and recovery feels poor, start the eating plan's second Shake on lifting days and give that lift one more round of the pair before you reset it (this plan's rule).

## When to deload

There's no scheduled deload during the novice run, because resets do that job. Take a light week only when a trigger shows up:

- Two or more barbell lifts at step 2 or later of the ladder at the same time
- Short nights (under 7 hours on most nights) at two lifting sessions in a row, or a week that's already overloaded
- Warm-ups that feel heavy two sessions in a row

**Illness (this plan's rule).** With a fever, widespread aches, unusual fatigue, chest congestion, a hacking cough, or an upset stomach, don't train ([Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/fitness/expert-answers/exercise/faq-20058494)). Come back once you've gone 24 hours without a fever and without fever medicine, use the missed-training table below, and make the rest of that week a light week with no intervals. With only a runny nose, congestion, or a minor sore throat, you can train, but make it a light week and keep conditioning easy. Sick days don't count toward anything: no misses, no conditioning weeks, and no extra conditioning-day food.

**Light week (this plan's rule).** Keep the same sessions, but do 2 work sets instead of 3 at 90% of your last completed weights. The deadlift stays at 1 set, and power cleans drop to 3 sets of 3. Give chins and dips 10 lb more assistance for 2 rounds, and keep conditioning easy with no intervals. The next week, go back to the weights you last made with no jump, or to the ladder step a lift was on.

**Floor week (this plan's rule).** For weeks that blow up, lift twice on days that aren't back to back, doing the next two workouts in A/B order without chins and dips. Do 2 sets of 5 at your last completed weights (1 set of 5 for the deadlift, 3 sets of 3 for power cleans), with no conditioning. Floor sessions keep the A/B order but don't set jumps or count toward a stall, and the next week starts at the same weights. Like the eating plan's floor week, it holds the line.

Two hard weeks are already on the calendar. Plan Christmas week (December 21–25) and Holy Week (March 22–26, 2027) as floor weeks now. Christmas week is a floor week for lifting only, so eat on the maintenance-break pattern that week, with three Mains a day, and treat holiday meals as event meals. Also plan around Thanksgiving (Thursday, November 26), New Year's Day (Friday, January 1, 2027), and Ash Wednesday (February 10, 2027). If one costs a session, use the missed-day rule under The week.

**After missed training:**

| Time away | First session back |
| --- | --- |
| One missed session | Carry on with the next workout, at the weights it would have had |
| About a week (6 or 7 days) | Repeat your last weights, set for set, in A/B order |
| 8 days to 3 weeks | Drop 5% if you're under 40, 10% if you're 40 or over |
| 3 weeks to 2 months | Drop 10%, or 20% if the last warm-up feels heavy |
| More than 2 months | Start light again, at about 60% of your last weights |

Count time away in calendar days since your last lifting session. The rows for about a week and for 8 days to 3 weeks follow [Alter](https://startingstrength.com/article/the-reset-why-and-how). [Andy Baker](https://startingstrength.com/training/coming-back-after-a-layoff) drops about 20% after two weeks to a month away and starts very light after three months, so the last two rows split the difference. Chins and dips get 10 lb more assistance after 8 days away, or 20 lb after 3 weeks (this plan's rule).

**Away from the gym.** On a trip, walk briskly for 30 to 45 minutes on your usual conditioning days, and skip the extra food slot. Count the lifting as missed and use the table above when you're back (this plan's rule).

The eating plan's maintenance breaks are not deloads. Keep lifting normally through them, and push.

## Eating and sleep

Your Modular Eating System covers the food. Its calorie estimate already counts three lifting days a week. It doesn't count the conditioning, so this plan adds food on those days.

1. **Feed the conditioning.** Add a second Shake on days you do conditioning. From conditioning week 5, when those sessions run 40 minutes or more, make it a second Breakfast instead. Skip the extra on days you don't condition. That roughly matches what the sessions burn as they grow (this plan's estimate).
2. **Expect strength to hold up better than muscle.** Across studies, strength gains in a deficit were not significantly smaller than at maintenance, but muscle gain shrank, and a deficit near 500 calories a day stopped it on average ([Murphy and Koehler](https://portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/energy-deficiency-impairs-resistance-training-gains-in-lean-mass-/)). High protein offsets part of that. Young men in a steep deficit, eating 2.4 g/kg and training hard six days a week, still gained lean mass in 4 weeks ([Longland et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/)).
3. **Keep protein where it is.** Protein's added muscle gain leveled off around 1.6 g per kg of body weight in a large meta-analysis of healthy adults, mostly not dieting ([Morton et al.](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/6/376.abstract)). Your 180 g is about 1.5 g per kg. Body fat doesn't raise protein needs, so that's close enough, and the extra slots on conditioning days add more. Longland's 2.4 g/kg would be nearly 300 g a day at your weight, far more than this plan asks.
4. **Have a Shake after lifting.** The eating plan already suggests it there, and it anchors training days.
5. **Use the plan's lever before a reset.** If lifts stall early and recovery feels poor, the eating plan adds a second Shake on lifting days. Its other trigger, losing faster than 2 lb a week after the first month, catches whatever the conditioning slots miss.
6. **Push during maintenance breaks.** The eating plan adds two weeks at maintenance every eight, and those are your best weeks for breaking a stall. The first starts December 21, a week early, so it covers Christmas and runs through January 3. Christmas week stays a floor week, so push in the second week. The next break starts March 1.

Your plan's deficit, roughly 500 to 900 calories a day by its own estimate, sits at or past that 500-calorie mark. That suits a goal of losing fat while strength climbs. If adding muscle ever matters more than the pace of fat loss, a smaller deficit is the lever, not more protein.

Don't use conditioning to pay for food. The eating plan makes the same rule about extra cardio after a big night.

**Sleep at least 7 hours.** That's the adult minimum in the joint consensus statement of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society ([AASM](https://aasm.org/seven-or-more-hours-of-sleep-per-night-a-health-necessity-for-adults/)). A run of short nights is a deload trigger. After a single night under 6 hours, lift as planned but take the next smaller jump, and skip intervals that day (this plan's rule).

## What research on trained lifters adds

Trained and competitive lifters have been studied, mostly in 8- to 12-week trials with small groups, plus large sets of meet results. Long-term randomized work on elite lifters is what's thin. Rippetoe's own complaint about exercise science is that studies use loads too light to matter ([Rippetoe](https://startingstrength.com/article/the_problem_with_exercise_science)), which is true of some studies but not of the ones below.

| Finding | Who was studied | What it changes here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Undulating programs, which vary load within the week, beat linear ones for 1RM in trained lifters, a borderline result (P = 0.05), but not in untrained ones | 2022 meta-analysis of volume-matched trials ([Moesgaard et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/)) | Simple progression is enough for now. Once a lift leaves the novice run, vary its load across the week. |
| Strength gains barely depend on how close to failure sets end, and load matters more | 2024 meta-regressions ([Robinson et al.](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4089104?lng=en)) | Most work sets end with a rep or two left. |
| Strength rises with weekly sets and with frequency, but extra sets pay off less and less | 67 studies, 2,058 people ([Pelland et al.](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097139?lng=en)) | After the novice run, spread a lift across more days before adding sets. |
| RPE-based loading matched or slightly beat fixed percentages | 21 trained men over 8 weeks ([Helms et al.](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2018.00247/text)) | The reps-in-reserve table is how trained lifters load, not a beginner's crutch. |
| Powerlifters gained on 3–6 heavy sets of 1–5 reps per lift each week, and adding 2–3 back-off sets to a top single may gain more | Review of powerlifting studies, a preprint ([Androulakis-Korakakis et al.](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/80/119/100)) | A single floor week won't cost strength. A heavy top set with back-off sets is a lean option after the novice run. |
| The strongest male powerlifters gained more slowly than weaker ones | 1,897 competitors over 15 years ([Latella et al.](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/88470/long-term-strength-adaptation-a-15-year-analysis-of-powerlifting-athletes)) | Progress slows as you get strong, so past the novice run, sustainable beats fast. |

The novice effect shows up in the data too. In untrained lifters, linear and undulating programs produced the same gains ([Moesgaard et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/)), which supports running a simple progression first.
